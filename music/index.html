Music of the Book

The songs that travelled with us

Music has a way of carrying memories across generations. A few familiar notes can take us back to a room, a voice, or a moment we thought we’d forgotten.

Throughout Hameward Bend – A Family’s Journey Home, music is woven into the family’s story. There were songs sung by soldiers far from home, popular tunes played on the gramophone, melodies drifting from the wireless, and the songs that became part of everyday life at Armour Street.

Some spoke of longing and separation, others of love, laughter and the simple pleasure of being together. They belonged to different generations, but each has found its way into the pages of the book.

I’ve gathered some of those songs here, using recordings that bring us a little closer to the sounds of their time. Not every recording is the one my family would have heard, but the melodies are part of the world they knew.

So put the kettle on, turn up the music, and come back with me for a wee while.

The First World War — Songs of Soldiers and Home

The First World War gave rise to songs that were spirited, sentimental, humorous and sometimes heartbreakingly homesick. They were sung by men far from their families, helping them endure the uncertainty of war.

  • Ma! I Miss Your Apple Pie — The Jesters
    A song of homesickness and longing for the familiar comforts of home.
    From the chapter: Ma, I Miss Your Apple Pie
  • Pack Up Your Troubles in Your Old Kit-Bag — The Billy Watson Band & Singers
    A cheerful marching song that encouraged soldiers to keep smiling, however difficult their circumstances.
  • Oh! How I Hate to Get Up in the Morning — Irving Berlin
    A soldier’s humorous complaint about army life, captured here in Berlin’s later performance from This Is the Army (1943).
  • Over There — American Patriotic Song
    One of the best-known American songs of the First World War, reflecting the enthusiasm surrounding America’s entry into the conflict.

The 1920s and 1930s — The Gramophone Years

After the war came years of change, and music entered more homes through gramophone records and the wireless. Popular songs provided an escape from daily worries, bringing romance, humour and entertainment into ordinary households.

  • Let the Rest of the World Go By — Charles Hart and Elliott Shaw (1918 recording)
    A tender song about finding happiness together, away from the cares of the world.
    From the chapter: Just a Spot to Call Our Own
  • Cheek to Cheek — Fred Astaire and Ginger Rogers (1935)
    The glamour and elegance of the cinema in the 1930s, when a song and a dance could transport audiences into another world.

Al Jolson — Songs from the Scullery

Al Jolson’s records were among the popular music remembered from family life. These lively, sentimental songs evoke an era when the gramophone provided entertainment at home.

  • Baby Face — Al Jolson
  • Angel Child — Al Jolson
  • Toot, Toot, Tootsie! (Goo’ Bye) — Al Jolson
  • Oh, You Beautiful Doll — Al Jolson

These recordings accompany the memories collected in From the Scullery.

The Second World War — Waiting and Remembering

Once again, music gave voice to the experiences of people separated by war. Songs of hope, love and reunion carried particular meaning for those waiting at home and those serving overseas.

  • The White Cliffs of Dover — Vera Lynn
    A song of hope for peace and the return of loved ones.
    From the chapter: Bet’s War
  • I’ll Be Seeing You — Frank Sinatra with Tommy Dorsey and His Orchestra
    A song that beautifully captures the longing for someone absent, and the memories that keep them close.
    From the chapter: The Other Frank

The 1950s — A New Generation

The war was over, and the years that followed brought new music, new fashions and different possibilities. The songs of the 1950s became part of the soundtrack to another generation’s childhood.

  • Mr. Wonderful — Peggy Lee (1956)
    A romantic favourite from the 1950s, recalling the music that accompanied family life in the post-war years.
    From the chapter: Newfield Square

Music Across the Generations

The songs gathered here span more than half a century. Some were sung in the trenches, some played on gramophones, and others came into our homes through the wireless and the cinema.

They remind me that family history is not only preserved in photographs, certificates and letters. Sometimes it survives in a tune we can still hum, or in the sound of a song we haven’t heard for fifty years.

The voices may be gone, but the music remains.
